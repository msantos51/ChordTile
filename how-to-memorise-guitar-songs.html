How to memorise guitar songs (and stop forgetting them)

You can play the riff while the tab is open. Close it and it’s gone. You know the intro of fifty songs and the whole of none. This isn’t a bad memory: it’s the way most of us practise. Here is why it happens, and a method that works with any tab, for guitar or bass.

Why you forget songs as soon as you close the tab

  • Reading is not remembering. With the tab open, your eyes do the work and your memory never has to. You can play a song fifty times this way and still not know it.
  • The whole song is too much at once. A tab is hundreds of notes. Trying to keep all of it in your head in one go is what makes it slip. Small pieces are much easier to hold.
  • You never test yourself. What makes something stick is trying to recall it, with nothing to look at, and getting it a little wrong. That effort is the practice.
  • You don’t come back to it. A song you learnt in a weekend and never played again fades by Friday. A short review the next day keeps it.

The method: one section at a time

You don’t memorise a song; you memorise a few sections and the order they come in. Most songs repeat their parts (verse, chorus, verse, chorus…), so there is less to learn than the tab suggests.

  1. Map the song. Before playing a note, list the sections in order: Intro, Verse, Chorus, Verse, Chorus, Bridge… Notice which ones repeat and which chords each one uses. This map is what you will remember the song by.
  2. Learn one section with the tab. Loop just that section, slowly (50 or 75% speed is fine), until you can play it cleanly while reading. Don’t move on yet.
  3. Play it without the tab. Close it and play the section from memory. Stuck? Look for a second, close it again and carry on. Aim for three clean runs in a row without looking.
  4. Join it to what you know. From the second section on, play it straight after the ones you have already learnt, without the tab: 1 → 2, then 1 → 2 → 3. The joins are where songs usually fall apart, so practise them on purpose.
  5. Play the whole song, no tab. The last join is the whole song, start to finish, from memory.
  6. Review it the next day. Before opening the tab, try to play the song from memory. Look only at the parts you missed. Do it again a few days later, and it stays.

One section per session is plenty. Short, regular sessions beat one long one.

Tips that make it stick

  • Remember the chords, not every fret. “F, G, C, Am” is easier to keep than a row of numbers. Once you know the chord of each bar, the notes have something to hang on to.
  • Say it out loud. Saying the chord names or humming the riff while you play links the sound to the shape.
  • Count the repeats. “Chorus twice, the second time with the long ending” is the kind of detail you forget. Write short reminders like x2 or slide next to the bar.
  • Isolate the hard bar. If one bar keeps tripping you up, loop just that bar slowly, then put it back in its section.
  • Speed up gradually. Memorise it slowly, then raise the speed step by step until you can play along with the recording.
  • Play along with the band. Once you know it, mute your part and play over the rest. It is the best test that you really know the song.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I forget songs as soon as I close the tab?

Because with the tab open you are reading, not remembering: your eyes do the work and your memory never has to. To remember a song, you have to practise recalling it, with the tab closed, a small part at a time.

How long does it take to memorise a song?

It depends on the song and on your level, but because most songs repeat a few sections, you usually memorise a handful of parts, not the whole tab. Learning one section per session and reviewing the next day works better than one long session.

Should I learn by ear instead of with tabs?

Both help. The tab tells you where to put your fingers; listening tells you how it should sound. Use the tab to learn a section, then rely on the sound and your memory to play it without the tab.

Does this work for bass too?

Yes. The method is the same for guitar and bass, and for any part you learn from a tab.
